# Atelier Cendre — Color & Design Theory

## Purpose
This document defines the visual thinking behind Atelier Cendre: a contemporary art gallery identity designed to feel archival, quiet, and materially aware. The system supports exhibition-led storytelling rather than retail or marketing language.

## Color Theory

### The palette’s role
The palette is built around the sensation of aged paper, charcoal ink, and a restrained rust note. It keeps the artwork in control while giving the interface a recognizable institutional voice.

### Warm paper base
The warm off-white page ground functions like a gallery catalogue or conservation paper. It reduces the clinical effect of pure white and makes dark text feel softer and more tactile. It is used for editorial reading surfaces and the exhibition introduction.

### Charcoal anchor
Deep charcoal is the structural tone. It grounds the footer, creates strong reading contrast, and gives the page an evening-gallery atmosphere without relying on pure black. It is used for primary display typography and the visit area.

### Rust accent
A muted rust red acts as a curatorial marker, not a decorative color. It is reserved for indexes, dates, labels, and request links. This limited application gives small navigational details a sense of importance while preserving the artwork’s authority.

### Artwork as dominant color field
The cover image is intentionally the most colorful part of the page. Interface colors do not compete with it; instead, they echo its warmth. This makes each exhibition image feel like the page’s temporary palette leader.

### Contrast and legibility
Light type over the cover is used only at large scale or with adequate visual separation. Charcoal type sits on the paper surface for comfortable reading. Supporting text uses a softer dark neutral to establish hierarchy without becoming faint.

## Design Theory

### Curatorial hierarchy
The site is organized as a sequence of visual permissions:
1. Encounter the artwork.
2. Notice the exhibition title and artist.
3. Read the curatorial framing.
4. Find practical visit information.

This order mirrors an in-gallery experience: seeing comes before explanation.

### Full-bleed opening
The opening image occupies the first encounter to create scale and atmosphere. Sparse corner navigation prevents the interface from becoming a graphic overlay. Exhibition information is held low in the frame so it reads as a caption within the artwork, not a promotional headline.

### Editorial typography
The high-contrast serif creates a cultural and printed quality, suitable for exhibition titles and invitations. The neutral sans-serif provides a disciplined counterpoint for labels, dates, navigation, and logistics. The serif carries expression; the sans-serif carries instruction.

### Spacing as pacing
Large areas of open space slow the visitor down. The exhibition section is intentionally broad and calm, giving the text room to function like a wall label rather than product copy. Consistent margins form an invisible grid across the page.

### Asymmetry with control
The exhibition area uses a small index column and a wider text column. This creates a subtle editorial imbalance: functional metadata sits apart from the main proposition, helping visitors scan the page without interrupting the reading rhythm.

### Surface transitions
The site moves from artwork to paper to charcoal. These changes create three distinct modes:
- Immersion: the artwork cover.
- Reflection: the warm-paper exhibition note.
- Orientation: the dark visit footer.

Each surface gives a different type of content its appropriate setting.

### Detail discipline
Rules, labels, and icons are intentionally small. Their job is to orient, not decorate. Repeating letter spacing and restrained icon sizing gives the gallery an institutional coherence.

## Applying the System
- Let exhibition artwork lead visual color decisions.
- Use the rust accent only for metadata and purposeful calls to action.
- Keep editorial copy concise and spatially generous.
- Reserve the serif for titles and identity moments.
- Keep functional text in the sans-serif, with compact uppercase labels where appropriate.
- Avoid introducing bright interface colors or card-heavy layouts that compete with the artwork.
